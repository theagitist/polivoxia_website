She Came Down from the Mountains

A Zine in Two Volumes by Adri M. · GRSJ 515, Winter 2026
To Maruca and Anita, for the resilience.
To Tacha and Lore, also for the stories.
To Zoe, for the future.
And to all the women before them.

About the Project

This project is an attempt to trace and recover a lineage disrupted by colonial displacement. It traces five generations of women from the stolen Huetar land of Itskazú and Acserrí, in so-called Costa Rica, to Turtle Island, and functions as a fractured archive: a counter-archive that refuses a single voice and insists that the fragments of these lives are just as historically significant as the settler ledgers that tried to erase them.

Traditions of storytelling are integrally linked to gatherings of commemoration, protest, and resistance. Amahl Bishara, Speaking Across Generations and Geographies of the Ongoing Nakba at Seventy-six

The two volumes are in conversation with each other. Vol. 1 is the testimony; Vol. 2 is the reasoning behind it. Neither is complete without the other. Together, they form a single argument: that an Indigenous archive does not need institutional permission to exist, and that the act of making it is itself a form of resistance.

Vol. 1: The Witness

The Witness holds the voices, the faces, the names. It opens with Maruca, a woman who came down from the land of Chief Acserrí in the 1920s, whose real name was never told, who was taken into indentured servitude by the criollo Zúñiga Gutiérrez family, and whose daughter Anita was born in 1931 into the same violence. From there it follows the thread across five generations (Maruca, Anita, Lorena, Natacha, Zoe), tracing how silence, secrets, and so-called Christian charity reproduced the eliminatory logic of settler colonialism across a single household, and how the women endured anyway.

This volume performs what Gloria Anzaldúa calls theory in the flesh: the physical realities of Maruca's indentured servitude and Anita's torture become the basis for a broader critique of the Costa Rican settler state. It is an archive in three voices, a polyvoxia, each with its own colour palette and typography: the Voice of the Land (Fraunces, burnt ochre), the Voice of the Colonial (Courier Prime, slate blue), and the Voice of the Witness (Atkinson Hyperlegible). The zine mixes narrative prose, concrete poetry, an ASCII word-portrait hiding the full text of the piece, family photographs, and direct Spanish testimony.

Vol. 2: The Critique

The Critique is the analytical anchor of the project. It provides the academic and theoretical framework for the creative choices made in Volume 1, and explains why those voices were designed the way they were, why fragmentation was chosen over linear narrative, why three typographies instead of one.

Drawing on This Bridge Called My Back (Moraga & Anzaldúa), Theresa Hak Kyung Cha's Dictee, Claudia Rankine's Citizen, Kawika Guillermo's Nimrods, Y-Dang Troeung's Landbridge, Mai Der Vang's Yellow Rain, and the world-building of the Indigenomicon, the volume works through fragmentation as form, the three-voice polyvoxia, approaching trauma with tenderness, and a brutalist visual methodology designed to look like archival artifacts. Following Troeung, it pairs the horror of reproductive genocide with the continuity of life, refusing to dwell exclusively in the wound so that the archive can hold space for both grief and agency.

We need to tell our stories to each other, even when it hurts. Amahl Bishara, Speaking Across Generations and Geographies of the Ongoing Nakba at Seventy-six
